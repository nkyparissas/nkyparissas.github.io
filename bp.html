Branch predictor lab

A cycle-level model of an in-order front-end driving the direction predictors of Section III (III-A to III-E) of Mittal's survey (arXiv:1804.00261), each drawn in the notation of its figure in the paper. gselect, gshare, gskew and GEHL have no figure there, so they borrow the notation of the others.

Using it

  • Write C on the right (or load an example) and press Compile. Conditional branches get labels (B0, B1, …) that the gutter, the assembly, the log, the figures and the per-branch table all share.
  • Step advances one cycle. Play runs at the chosen speed; up to 16 cycles/s every cycle is animated and logged. Run to end goes flat out.
  • Knobs you have moved show their value in red; hover any knob to see its default. Defaults puts all of the selected predictor's knobs back (it is greyed out while they already are).
  • Changing the predictor or any knob restarts the program. Each run lands in Runs, so the same code can be compared across predictors and sizes. Data from rand() is seeded identically every run.

Reading the figures

  • Tables are drawn as in the paper: a few rows, a dashed gap and the last row. They never show contents. The fill colour is the table's stress, from blue (relaxed) and green (healthy) through yellow and orange to red (thrashing).
  • The solid entry is the one read for the prediction made this cycle; the purple dashed entry is the one trained by the branch that resolved this cycle. These are usually different branch instances.
  • Wires: blue carries the prediction, purple dashes the update from Execute, red the Execute redirect after a mispredict, orange a Decode redirect after a BTB miss. Faded wires carried nothing this cycle.
  • Hatched pipeline stages hold wrong-path instructions: fetched after a branch that is going to turn out mispredicted.
  • Zoom either figure with the − and + buttons beside its title, or Ctrl + scroll (pinch on a trackpad) over it. Zoomed in, drag the figure to pan. Double-click it, or click the percentage, to fit it to the panel again.

How stress and aliasing are measured

The modelled tables are tag-less, like the paper's, so hardware could not tell who collided. The simulator keeps a shadow owner per entry (the branch, or for per-set tables the code block, that last wrote it) purely to report it:

  • History tables: share of lookups landing on a row last written by another owner (for tagged tables: evicting it).
  • Pattern tables: every branch also trains a private copy of each counter it uses (again a shadow, not hardware). Interference is destructive when the shared counter mispredicted but the private one would have been right, and constructive the other way round (the survey's negative and positive aliasing, Section II-A). The colour follows the destructive rate, so a heavily shared global PHT can still be healthy.
  • Where no single counter decides (GEHL's sum, gskew's vote), a table's sharing counts as destructive only if the branch's private counter in its place would have turned a wrong sum or vote into a right one.
  • TAGE's tables are tagged, so the shadow owner exposes false tag hits. A tagged table's colour is the worse of two signals: false hits that cost a prediction, and allocation pressure (mispredictions that found its entry still useful). Agree's biasing bit table, like a tagged history table, counts lookups that evict another branch's bit.

The front-end

  • In order, one instruction per cycle; branches resolve in the last stage, so a mispredict costs depth − 1 cycles.
  • A predicted-taken branch needs its target from the BTB; a BTB miss is repaired at Decode (2 cycles).
  • Predictors that read two tables in series (the P and S variants, MAs in Fig. 5(a)) take two cycles, so each predicted-taken branch costs one fetch bubble. Fig. 5(b) removes it. The III-B to III-E predictors read their tables in parallel and are modelled with one-cycle lookups (GEHL and TAGE as if ahead-pipelined, as the survey notes for GEHL).
  • History is updated speculatively at fetch and repaired on a flush, or (knob) only at resolve. GEHL and TAGE keep hundreds of history bits and fold them into each table's index and tag width; a flush restores those folds too.

Language

int variables and arrays, if/else, for, while, do/while, break, continue, ?:, && and || (each a real branch), arithmetic, bitwise and comparison operators, and rand(lo, hi) (also as an array initialiser). Loops are compiled test-at-the-bottom, as gcc does. No function calls: calls and returns need target prediction, which the survey leaves out.
